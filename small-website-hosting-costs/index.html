$2.99 a month, four years up front

Figures read 21 September 2026; paid checkouts re-checked 26 September 2026. Amounts are in the currency each checkout displayed. By Jeff Peckham.

On 21 September I went to buy the cheapest shared hosting plan I could find, and stopped at the last screen before payment.

The plan was advertised at $2.99 a month. My cart said $153.57 due today. Both numbers were correct. The plan is sold on a 48-month term, paid up front: $2.99 is the pricing page's four-year total, $143.52, divided by forty-eight, and my cart came to $10.05 more than that, a difference I will come back to.

Five days later I went back to the same plan and got a different cart. This time the checkout showed Canadian dollars: CAN$4.19 a month, and CAN$215.20 due today, with the tax on its own line. So the figure due today is not a number I can hand you. It depends on where you are, what currency you are shown and when you look, and yours may well differ from both of mine. What did not move between the two visits was the shape: one payment, covering four years, made before the site has served a single page.

Before going further, I should say what I did not find, because it is the thing most articles about cheap hosting lead with. On 21 September I looked at three checkouts — Hostinger, SiteGround and DreamHost — and in all three the renewal price was shown before payment. It was shown again in all three when I went back on the 26th. On the first visit, no add-ons were pre-ticked in any of them. Every optional extra required me to click something. Whatever else is going on here, nobody was hiding the renewal rate or sneaking a backup subscription into my basket.

So the interesting thing is not a trick. It is the structure of the price: how long a term you are committing to, how much leaves your account today, what the price becomes afterwards, and for how long. Those four things vary a lot between hosts, and the advertised monthly figure tells you almost nothing about them.

The other half of this article is the part I did not expect to be able to write. Free static hosting now covers considerably more of a small site's needs than its price suggests — including, for many people, a working contact form and email at your own domain. It also comes with use restrictions, limits, and service guarantees that are genuinely different from what you get when you pay. Those differences are the real comparison.

Who this is for, and who it isn't

This article is about static sites: pages, text, images, links, and a contact form. A brochure site for a practice or a shop. A small publication. A course page. Something that does not need to compute anything when a visitor arrives.

If you need any of the following, you have a different problem and this is not your article:

  • WordPress, or anything running server-side PHP
  • a database
  • an e-commerce platform that takes payments
  • a managed site builder like Squarespace or Wix
  • any server-side application with a persistent runtime

There is no shame in needing those. Most small businesses do. But the free answers below exist precisely because a static site asks so little of a server, and they stop working the moment you need more. If you are not sure which you are, a reasonable test: if your site could be printed out and still make sense, it is probably static.

What the paid quote actually costs

Here is what the three checkouts showed on 21 September 2026, and then what they showed when I went back on 26 September. On 21 September I inspected each up to, but not through, the payment step. On 26 September I went through the checkout flows again. On neither date did I submit an order or buy anything.

Hostinger advertises its Premium plan at $2.99/mo, against a $11.99/mo reference price. The term is 48 months. On 21 September my cart put $153.57 due today. The renewal line said: "Renews after 48 months at $10.99/mo for 12 months." Read that twice. The initial term is four years; the renewal term is one. So after the single up-front payment, the plan renews at about $132 a year. The cart also included a free domain for the first year and two mailboxes of 1 GB each, free for the first year.

On 26 September the same plan came up in Canadian dollars: CAN$4.19/mo on the same 48-month term, a hosting subtotal of CAN$201.12, the domain and domain privacy at CAN$0, CAN$14.08 in tax, itemised, and CAN$215.20 due today. The renewal line had exactly the same shape: after 48 months, CAN$15.39/mo for 12 months — about CAN$185 a year. The free first-year domain and the two free-for-a-year 1 GB mailboxes were there again, and the Hostinger Ecommerce add-on was not selected. So I have now read the four-years-in, one-year-out renewal structure in two separate checkouts, in two currencies. That part is not an accident of one session. (One thing I cannot give you for the first visit is its currency: my record of that cart has the amounts with a plain dollar sign and no currency label, so I am not going to guess which dollar it was.)

There is one thing about the 21 September cart I still cannot account for. The pricing page states $143.52 for that 48-month term. The cart said $153.57 — $10.05 more — and showed no tax line, no subtotal and no breakdown of any kind. It happens to equal 7% of $143.52, which matches a sales-tax rate that applies where I was browsing from, but no Hostinger page I read says so. The 26 September checkout labelled CAN$14.08 as tax, and that is 7% of its CAN$201.12 hosting subtotal — the same proportion as the earlier gap. I am recording that match as an observation, not an explanation. It is a different session, in a different currency, and it does not tell me what the first cart was charging. I am not going to tell you the first difference was tax when that page did not. It remains unexplained, and I have left it that way.

SiteGround advertises StartUp at $2.99/mo against $17.99/mo, prepaid for 12 months — $35.88 due today, before VAT. On 21 September prices were marked "Ex. VAT" and the form required a VAT/Tax ID, so what you pay may be higher than the figure on screen. Renewal is at $17.99/mo, and the confirmation text said services "are set to auto-renew at regular price for the same period they were initially ordered." Same term, roughly six times the price.

The 26 September checkout was the same in every figure: $2.99/mo, 12 months, a $35.88 initial bill in US dollars against a $17.99/mo regular price, and an order summary of $35.88 with no tax added. The terms read the same too: discounted prices apply to the first invoice only, regular prices apply to renewals unless stated otherwise, and services auto-renew at the regular price for the period originally ordered. Nothing had moved.

On 21 September SiteGround was the only one of the three where I could see the whole itemised checkout without creating an account, and it was also the only one that told me what the free domain will cost later: the .com line showed "1 year FREE" next to "$22.99/yr". That is a useful thing to put in front of someone before they buy.

Its optional extras are worth understanding, because they are structured differently from the others. Site Scanner and Premium Backup are each $4.99/mo, offered as a one-month free trial. The small print under each says: "You'll be billed 5 days before your trial ends for 12 months at 50% off ($2.49/mo, $29.88 total)." Nothing was pre-ticked — on 21 September I checked the actual form controls, and all four upsells were off, as was the marketing-email box. But if you do tick one, a "one-month free trial" becomes a prepaid twelve-month charge unless you cancel inside roughly twenty-five days. That is a real thing to know, and it is disclosed; it is just disclosed in small type under a large word that says FREE.

On 21 September DreamHost showed its Launch plan at $34.68 for the first year, on a yearly term, "Renews automatically until canceled", with the cart stating "Renews at $131.88 yearly." Taxes are shown as "Calculated after billing address" — so, again, not the final number. Nothing was pre-selected. Professional Email is offered as an optional add-on at three months free, then $19.99/year for a 25 GB mailbox.

On 26 September the figures were the same: $2.89/mo, $34.68 for the first year, $131.88 a year on renewal (about $10.99 a month), renewing automatically until cancelled, tax calculated after the billing address, and Professional Email at three months free, then $19.99/year for one mailbox. One thing had changed. The cart's web address on 21 September carried a promotional code, and the 26 September checkout did not. The prices were identical without it. I cannot tell you what the code did, only that the prices above are what the checkout showed without it.

One DreamHost figure I could not pin down: the plan card lists "~40k Visits/mo" alongside "Unmetered Bandwidth". The tilde suggests guidance rather than a cap, but nothing I read said which it is, so I have not treated it as a limit.

What free static hosting actually gives you

Four providers give away static hosting that is not a trial and does not expire. They are not equivalent, and the differences that matter are mostly in the terms rather than the specifications.

Cloudflare is the one I have actually used, and I need to split it in two before saying anything else. There is Cloudflare Pages, which is what this site runs on, and there is Workers Static Assets, which is Cloudflare's newer way of doing the same job. The Pages documentation now opens with a banner: "Are you sure you want to use Pages? Workers supports most Pages use cases and offers a broader feature set. It is Cloudflare's primary platform for building applications. Start new projects with Workers."

So Cloudflare is pointing new users away from the product I have hands-on experience with. It has not said Pages is deprecated, and I found no end-of-life date, no migration requirement and no feature freeze. But "start new projects with Workers" is the vendor's own current advice, and I am not going to tell you to ignore it because I happen to be on the older one.

On limits, Pages Free gives 500 builds a month, one at a time, 20,000 files per site, a 25 MiB ceiling on any single file, and up to 100 projects. There is no bandwidth figure on the Pages limits page at all. The Workers pricing page says "Requests to static assets are free and unlimited" and that there are no charges for egress or bandwidth — but that egress sentence sits inside the description of the paid Workers plan, so I am not going to quote it as a statement about the free tier. What I can say is that no bandwidth limit for Pages Free is published where limits are published.

On terms, Cloudflare's Service-Specific Terms include Pages and Workers in a "Developer Platform" section that says, plainly: "Unlike most Cloudflare products, the Developer Platform can be used to host content." I found no commercial-use restriction in either that document or the subscription agreement. I am not going to turn that silence into permission — but it is a meaningful contrast with two of the three providers below, which restrict commercial use in writing.

GitHub Pages is the most-recommended free option and the one most often described wrongly. It does carry a restriction, and here it is in full:

GitHub Pages is not intended for or allowed to be used as a free web-hosting service to run your online business, e-commerce site, or any other website that is primarily directed at either facilitating commercial transactions or providing commercial software as a service (SaaS).

Read what that actually says. It targets sites primarily directed at commercial transactions or at providing SaaS. It does not say "no business may have a website here." Whether a brochure site for a plumber — no payments, no cart, just hours and a phone number — falls inside it is genuinely unclear to me, and I am not going to decide it on GitHub's behalf. If your site takes money, this is not the place. If it is a business card, the honest answer is that the wording does not settle it.

The published limits: 1 GB per published site, a 10-minute deploy timeout, a 100 GB/month soft bandwidth limit, and a soft limit of 10 builds an hour which does not apply if you deploy via your own GitHub Actions workflow. What happens if you exceed them is unusually mild: "we may not be able to serve your site, or you may receive a polite email from GitHub Support." No bill.

Netlify has moved to a credit system, and the free tier is now 300 credits a month, described as a "Hard limit." Credits are consumed by production deploys (15 each), bandwidth (20 per GB) and web requests (2 per 10,000). Twenty deploys is 300 credits. That is tighter than it first looks for a site you are actively working on.

What happens when you run out depends on which Netlify document you read, and this is the one contradiction in this article I cannot resolve for you. The current credit documentation says: "Once your credit balance is completely used up, all of your web projects (sites/apps) are paused and visitors to your web projects will find a Site not available page." Note all your projects, not just the one that went over. But Netlify's binding Self-Serve Subscription Agreement, last updated 1 October 2025, says something else about the same situation: "if use exceeds the Netlify Services capacity applicable to the Free Usage Tier, Customer will incur and agrees to pay additional fees that reflect actual usage."

One document says your site goes dark. The other says you agree to pay. They cannot both be describing the same event, and I am not going to pick one and present it as the answer. What I can tell you is that the credit documentation also says credit packs and auto-recharge are available only on paid plans, and that auto-recharge is off by default — so there is no purchase mechanism on the free tier for the fees the agreement contemplates.

That same agreement is worth reading before you put a business site on the free tier for other reasons. It describes the Free Usage Tier as existing "to allow users to experience Netlify Services", offered "at Netlify's sole discretion", running in a shared build environment "with no service level commitments". It states that Netlify "may shut down Free Usage Tier website projects without notice for any reason or no reason" — and that where there are performance problems, "including those caused by a malicious attack on a website project, for the Free Usage Tier Netlify will resolve the issue by shutting down the affected website projects." Commercial use itself is fine; the licence is granted for the customer's "internal business purposes." It is the service guarantee that is absent, and it is absent explicitly.

Vercel is the simplest case. Its Hobby plan is free and its fair-use guidelines say: "Hobby teams are restricted to non-commercial personal use only. All commercial usage of the platform requires either a Pro or Enterprise plan." Commercial usage is defined broadly — "any Deployment that is used for the purpose of financial gain of anyone involved in any part of the production of the project" — with examples including taking payment, advertising the sale of a product or service, being paid to build or host the site, carrying advertising, or affiliate linking as the site's primary purpose. Asking for donations is explicitly not commercial.

For most readers of this article, that settles it: if the site exists to support a business, Vercel's free tier is not available to you. (Confusingly, the same page lists "Blogs, ecommerce, and marketing sites" as examples of fair use. Those are two different questions — what the platform is technically for, and which plan you must be on — but they sit close enough together to mislead.)

The free stack

You can assemble a working small site for the price of a domain name and nothing else. Static hosting from any of the above, Web3Forms for the contact form, and Zoho Mail's Forever Free plan for email at your own domain.

Web3Forms gives 250 submissions a month, free permanently, with no credit card. It works as an ordinary HTML form that posts to their endpoint — no JavaScript required, which matters if you want a site that works everywhere and loads instantly. It includes honeypot spam filtering rather than making you bolt on a CAPTCHA.

Zoho Mail's free plan is the load-bearing piece, and it comes with a qualifier that has to travel with it. It is real mailbox hosting — not forwarding — for one domain and up to five users, 5 GB each, and Zoho's usage policy states the service is "mainly intended for personal and normal business email usage." But Zoho also states, on at least four of its own pages, that the free plan is "available only in select data centers" and does not publish which ones. I could not establish whether it is available in any particular country, including my own. You will find out by trying to sign up. I am not able to tell you it will work where you are.

Three more things about it, all of which I would want to know before building on it. Zoho's terms allow termination of unpaid accounts inactive for 120 days, with the data deleted — which is a live risk for a contact@ address on a quiet site. Zoho's pricing page says IMAP and POP are not included on the free plan, while three of Zoho's own configuration help pages document free-tier POP, IMAP and SMTP server settings; I could not reconcile those, so do not count on reading your mail in a desktop client. And while normal business correspondence is permitted, Zoho Mail explicitly prohibits newsletters, marketing, promotional, bulk, automated and transactional email — those are meant to go through separate Zoho products. If you plan to send a newsletter, this is not the tool for it.

The email fork: a mailbox or forwarding, not both

This is the part most guides get wrong, and it is worth being precise.

A mailbox is somewhere your mail lives. Forwarding takes mail addressed to you and relays it into a mailbox you already have somewhere else. They are not the same thing, and for one domain you must choose.

The reason is mundane: mail delivery for a domain is controlled by its MX records, and a domain has one set of them. Zoho's setup instructions say to "remove any existing entries that you might have" and that "only the MX Records provided in the Tools & Configurations section of Admin Console should be used." Cloudflare Email Routing, meanwhile, publishes its own MX servers for your domain and additionally requires that your DNS be hosted at Cloudflare. Both want the same record. You can have Zoho's mailbox or Cloudflare's forwarding on a given domain — not both.

And if you choose forwarding, there is a consequence that is easy to miss until it has already happened in front of a customer. Cloudflare documents it plainly:

Email Routing does not support sending or replying from your Cloudflare domain. When you reply to emails forwarded by Email Routing, the reply will be sent from your destination address (like my-name@gmail.com), not from the email pattern of the routing rule that delivered the message (like info@yourdomain.com).

So mail arrives at hello@yourbusiness.com, and your reply goes out from your personal Gmail. Free forwarding gets you a professional address for receiving. It does not get you one for answering.

One dated thing: Gmail's "Send as" ends in January 2027

If your plan was to keep everything in Gmail and just send as your domain address, note the date.

Google is removing the "Send as" feature for third-party addresses in January 2027. Its support documentation states the change "only applies to third-party, non-Google email accounts." Gmail addresses and Google Workspace aliases are unaffected. A mailbox at Zoho — or at any provider that is not Google — is a third-party account, so it is covered.

Google's published timeline: notice from Q3 2026; a transition period through Q3–Q4 2026 during which the feature keeps working but "Gmail may restrict new configurations"; full removal in January 2027, along with Gmailify and POP fetching from other accounts.

Two things this does not affect, which are easy to conflate. Adding an entire third-party account to the Gmail mobile app is explicitly listed as unchanged, and Google names that app as one of the recommended ways forward. And third-party clients like Outlook, Thunderbird or Apple Mail can still reach Gmail over IMAP and POP. What goes away is the specific arrangement where you sit in your Gmail account on the web and send as an address hosted elsewhere.

This is a scheduling problem, not a catastrophe. But if you are choosing an email setup now, it is worth choosing one that still works in eighteen months. Google also warns against browser extensions offering to replicate the feature, on the grounds that they typically require your credentials or OAuth tokens.

What paying can buy

Not every paid host provides all of these, and some of them you can get for free elsewhere. But this is the honest list of what money can be buying in this category:

  • server-side capability — PHP, WordPress, anything that runs on request
  • a database and a persistent runtime
  • bundled email at your domain, set up for you
  • an easier workflow — a control panel and one-click installs instead of a deploy pipeline
  • support — somebody to ask
  • service commitments — an actual guarantee that the site stays up
  • higher or more predictable limits
  • reduced operational complexity — one vendor, one bill, one place where things are configured

That last one is worth more than it looks. The free stack in this article involves three separate services and a DNS configuration you have to get right. If assembling that is not something you want to spend a weekend on, paying someone to have already assembled it is a legitimate purchase, not a failure of nerve.

So: free, paid, or stay where you are?

Free makes sense when the site is genuinely static; the provider's commercial-use terms permit what you are doing; the published limits are comfortably above your traffic; you can live with the workflow and the absence of support; and you can handle email and forms as separate pieces.

Paying makes sense when you need server-side capability; a mailbox, support or workflow simplicity matters more to you than the price; predictable limits and a service commitment matter — for a site that takes bookings, "may be shut down for any reason or no reason" is a real risk; or when having one vendor handle everything reduces enough complexity to be worth the money.

Staying where you are makes sense when the migration effort and risk outweigh the saving; your email is tied to your current host and moving it endangers continuity; you are mid-term on something you have already prepaid and the remaining value is real; or the saving is small in absolute terms. On that last point, do the arithmetic in dollars per year rather than percentages. "Sixty per cent cheaper" and "seventy dollars a year" are the same fact, and only one of them helps you decide whether it is worth a weekend and some downtime.

If I had to compress this into one recommendation: for a genuinely static site with no payments on it, start free, and move to paid when a specific named thing forces you to — a database, a mailbox you want bundled, a support channel, or a service guarantee you actually need. The free tiers are capable enough that "I might need more later" is not by itself a reason to prepay four years up front today.

Which free host depends mostly on terms rather than features. If your site is a business site, Vercel's free tier is out by its own rules, and GitHub Pages is unclear enough that I would not build a commercial site on it without deciding you are comfortable with the ambiguity. Netlify permits business use but disclaims any service guarantee in unusually direct language. Cloudflare's developer platform terms explicitly permit hosting content and I found no commercial restriction in them — but see the disclosure below before weighing that, and note that Cloudflare is currently telling new users to start on Workers rather than on Pages.

What I could not establish

These are the questions I could not answer from the providers' own documentation. They stay here rather than being quietly dropped.

  • Zoho's free-plan regional eligibility. Restricted on four of Zoho's pages, listed on none.
  • Whether Zoho's free plan includes IMAP and POP. Zoho's pricing page and three of its configuration pages disagree.
  • What exceeding Netlify's free tier actually does. Its agreement says fees may be incurred; its current documentation says a hard limit and a pause.
  • Refund exclusions, and whether domain fees are refundable, at any of the three paid hosts.
  • The $10.05 difference between Hostinger's pricing page and my 21 September cart. A later checkout labelled 7% of its subtotal as tax, the same proportion; that is an observation, not an explanation of the earlier gap.
  • Whether DreamHost's "~40k Visits/mo" is a cap or guidance.
  • Netlify's Acceptable Use Policy, which its agreement incorporates by reference. I did not read it.
  • The long-term status of Cloudflare Pages beyond "start new projects with Workers".
  • Cloudflare Pages' free-tier bandwidth, which is not stated where its limits are stated.

I have also not tested whether any provider actually enforces its free-tier commercial-use rules, and I do not intend to. Finding out would mean deliberately breaking someone's terms to see what happens.

How this was researched, and one disclosure you should weigh

Every figure here was read from the provider's own pricing pages, documentation, terms or checkouts on 21 September 2026. On 26 September I went back through all three paid checkouts, and re-checked the four things most likely to have moved: Netlify's free allowance and credit structure, Cloudflare's advice to start new projects on Workers, Google's January 2027 date, and Zoho's free-plan availability wording. Those four had not changed. Where a checkout had, both dates are shown above, and the earlier figure is kept as what it was: an observation from that day, not the current price. Prices in this category go stale quickly; those two dates are the limit of what this article can tell you. Where two of a vendor's own pages disagreed, I have shown you the disagreement rather than picking the one that made a neater story.

On 21 September the three paid checkouts were inspected up to, but not through, the payment step, and no account was created; Hostinger and DreamHost both required one before showing the payment page, which is why some of their figures are missing above. On 26 September the checkout flows were inspected again. On neither date was an order submitted or anything bought.

And the disclosure. The Small Operator runs on Cloudflare Pages, and its contact address uses Cloudflare Email Routing to forward mail to a private inbox. Email Routing was set up after the research for this article was done, and nothing I say about it above comes from using it. The hands-on observations in this article come from Pages alone: one real publication, on one custom domain, deployed from GitHub, with DNS at Cloudflare, apex and www routing, a custom 404, canonical metadata and a sitemap. That is the whole of it. I did not test it under load, at scale, or across multiple projects. I never approached a bandwidth, storage or build limit, so I have no idea what happens when you do. I have never contacted their support. I cannot make an uptime claim from a window this short.

Cloudflare is the only hosting option in this comparison I have used, and its Email Routing is the forwarding option this publication now uses. There is no commercial relationship, nothing here earns a commission, and there are no affiliate links anywhere on this site. But you should still weigh that the product I know best is the product this page is served from — and better-evidenced is not the same as better. It is also worth repeating that Cloudflare's own documentation now points new projects at Workers rather than at Pages, which is the thing I actually use.
